Someone asks ChatGPT for a good accountant in their town, and it names three firms. Something decided those three. It wasn’t random, and it wasn’t an ad.

More people are starting this way. Instead of searching, scrolling, and judging for themselves, they ask an assistant and act on the short answer it gives back. Which means there’s a new question worth asking about your own business: when someone asks AI for what you do, in the place you do it, does your name come up?

## How does an AI assistant decide who to name?

It reads, and it leans hardest on the sources *other people* built: your Google reviews, your listings and directory profiles, the "best [whatever] in [town]" lists, the way you’re mentioned around the web. It stitches the businesses those sources vouch for into a short answer. This isn’t only my read on it. In one [industry survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/google-local-algorithm-and-ranking-factors/) of local-search specialists, being named on those expert "best of" lists came out as the single biggest factor behind AI visibility.

Your own site matters too, but its job here is narrower than people think. It’s how the tool confirms who you are and picks up the plain words to describe you. It’s rarely what gets you named on its own. A clear site helps the machine *talk about you correctly*; your reputation across the web is more of what decides whether it talks about you *at all*.

## I ran the test

I asked the assistants "who’s the best [trade] in Burlington?" for a handful of real local businesses, and the result was consistent enough to be worth saying plainly:

- A company with a bare, barely-built website came up **first**, because it had a wall of strong reviews and showed up on the local "best of" lists.
- A business with a clean, well-organized site and good reviews **didn’t come up at all**. The wider web just wasn’t pointing at it yet.

The pattern: the web’s opinion of you, more than your web design, is what the assistant repeats. That’s no knock on good websites, and a clear site is still doing real work. It’s just honest about which lever is bigger.

## What moves this, and what doesn’t

What doesn’t: a clever website on its own, more pages, or paying for a spot. You can’t buy your way into the recommendation itself, because being *named* as a good choice isn’t a slot that’s for sale. The same holds one layer down, in the listings these assistants lean on: [Google itself says](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) there’s no way to pay for a better local ranking. (As of September 2026, ads do appear inside and beside some AI answers, labelled as sponsored. That’s a separate thing you can pay for, and OpenAI says it doesn’t change ChatGPT’s answer itself. It’s not the same as the assistant recommending you.)

What does comes in two parts, and it’s worth being straight about both:

**The part on your own site, which you control.** Say what you do in plain words, near the top, where it can't be missed. Name the place you work and the kind of client you work with. Keep the way you're described consistent everywhere a machine might check, so the story doesn't contradict itself. This is the part I can fix directly, and it makes sure that when you *are* named, it's in your words.

**The bigger part, off your site, which has to be earned.** Real reviews, coming in steadily. Listings and profiles that all line up. Being present on the local lists and pages these tools actually read. Some of that is a quick fix: clean up your listings. Some of it takes time, and it isn't anyone's to guarantee. You can't manufacture reviews, and no one can force a "best of" list to add you. Anyone promising to *guarantee* you a spot in AI answers is selling something that isn't theirs to sell.

It’s the same idea as being easy to refer in person. The friend who sends you business can describe what you do in one sentence, and other people already speak well of you. The goal here is the same, just written where a machine can find it.

## Try this yourself: the ten-minute test

Open the assistant you use and ask it four questions, without your business name in any of them. The point is to see whether it recommends you to a stranger.

1. **The category question.** "Who are the best [what you do] in [your town]?"
2. **The specific-need question.** "Who can help me with [one thing you do] near [your area]?"
3. **The comparison question.** "Compare the top options for [what you do] in [your town]."
4. **The source question.** If you do come up, ask "where did you find that about [your business]?" It will tell you which pages it read, and that is the list of places your reputation lives.

Then ask the one branded question: "what does [your business name] do?" Read the answer back honestly. If it’s vague, or wrong, or empty, that’s no verdict on your business. It’s a fair picture of what the web is currently telling a machine about you, which is a thing you can work on.

## What my own check showed

I run this on my own site, and the results are worth sharing because they show two things a scanner never will.

On 1 September 2026 I asked the three assistants the same eight questions I ask for clients. For "best AI marketing consultants in Burlington," Perplexity and ChatGPT both named me. The next day, asked again in a browser, neither did. Same question, same engines, one day apart. The answer you get is a snapshot, and anyone who tells you where you "rank" in an AI answer is describing the weather.

The branded question moved the other way, and stayed moved. In early August, ChatGPT described me from a data-broker page and had my services wrong. By September all three assistants described what I sell correctly, with prices, and cited my own site. That change followed the work you can control: plain words on the site, a verified Google Business Profile, the same facts in every listing.

Google’s AI Mode is the one that has not named me for the category question on any day, and its answer shows why. It builds the recommendation from Business Profile cards, and the profiles it showed carried between 4 and 72 reviews. My profile was two weeks old with none. That isn’t a website problem, and no amount of rewriting the homepage would touch it. It’s the off-site part, and it is earned the slow way.

That small test will tell you more than any scanner. It’s worth ten minutes.
