There’s a moment after a new site launches when it feels done: signed off, live, ticked off the list. It’s a good feeling, and it’s also the start of something nobody mentions in the pitch. A website isn’t a thing you finish. It’s a thing you keep true.

That can sound like the setup for a maintenance contract. It isn’t. It’s just how being found works now, for Google and for the AI assistants people increasingly ask instead, and it’s worth understanding whether the upkeep is a job you do yourself or one you hand off.

## What happens when you stop touching it

A website doesn’t stop working the day you walk away from it. There’s no switch and no penalty timer. What happens is quieter, and it comes down to trust.

Both Google and the AI assistants lean toward sources they’ve had reason to rely on for a while. That trust is cumulative. It builds as a site stays accurate, consistent, and specific over time, and a site that goes silent simply stops adding to it. Meanwhile the web around you keeps moving: information dates, other businesses publish, and "the current best answer" drifts toward whoever is visibly still tending their corner. Every honest, specific page you add is also one more question you can be the clean answer to.

None of that is a crisis. It’s the difference between a site that quietly compounds and one that slowly goes stale.

## Keeping it true is lighter than it sounds

For a business without a marketing department, this is the part that matters: keeping a site alive is mostly keeping it *accurate*, rather than feeding a content machine.

A trades accountant doesn’t need a blog post a week. They need the site to still say the right thing when the business changes: a new service, a different focus, a second location. If the site says the old thing, the machine repeats the old thing. Past that, it’s the occasional real piece that answers a question a client actually asked. A few specific pages will do more than a lot of thin ones.

So the honest answer to "is it ever finished?" is no, but the upkeep is lighter and more human than the word "maintenance" suggests.
