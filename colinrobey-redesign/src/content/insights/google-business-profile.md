Someone needs a bookkeeper, or a plumber, or a physio. They pull out their phone and search. Near the top of what Google shows them, usually above any regular website link, sits a map and a short list of businesses, each with a star rating, a photo, and whether they're open right now.

That panel is built from your Google Business Profile. For a lot of local businesses it's the first impression, ahead of the website you paid real money for. And in most cases it's a record somebody filled in once, years ago, and hasn't opened since.

## What actually shows up when someone searches for you?

For anything with local intent, a "bookkeeper in Burlington" or a "landscaper near me," Google usually leads with Business Profiles: the map results, the panel beside a branded search, the stars, the photos, the hours. Ads can sit above all of it, and they're labelled as ads. The regular website links are further down, and plenty of people never get that far.

The AI side of search looks to be heading the same way. In the checks I've run on real local queries, Google's AI results answered with business-profile cards, showing each firm's name, star rating, review count, and whether it was open, before any website got a mention. The same record that fills the map panel was filling the AI answer.

Your website still matters. It's where someone lands once they've picked you, and for specific, detailed questions it can be the thing that wins you the answer. But for the first look, the profile is frequently what's on screen.

## How out of date is a typical profile?

Here's a real one I reviewed recently, for a business I work with. Google's own dashboard labelled it "Complete Info." The basics were genuinely fine: name, address, phone, hours, a current description.

Then the parts a customer reads. Services: empty, even though the firm sells three clearly named services and lists all of them on its website. Photos: the most recent upload was from 2018. Questions and answers: empty. Recent updates: none.

So the record Google was showing people said, in effect, "we exist," and nothing about what the business does. The "Complete Info" label only grades the basics.

I don't think that profile is unusual. It's what set-it-and-forget-it looks like, and forgetting is the default, because the profile isn't something an owner ever has a reason to open. It just sits there, answering for you every day.

## What does fixing it change — and what doesn't it?

The fixes come in two halves, and they're not the same kind of work.

The first half you control directly, and most of it is a one-time job: list your services in plain words, add current photos, check the categories, make sure the hours and phone number are right, seed the Q&A with the questions customers really ask. When that's done, the record Google shows says what you do, in your own words. That's worth having on its own.

The second half is earned, and it's slower. In every check I've run on the broad "best whatever near me" searches, the businesses holding those spots carried far more reviews than the ones that didn't. That's a pattern I keep seeing, not proof of cause, and reviews are one signal among several. But they're the one you can act on: they come from asking happy customers, steadily, over time. Nobody can install them for you, and a filled-in profile doesn't jump the queue. It makes you accurate and eligible, which is different from being chosen. As for buying your way up, you can pay for the ads around these results, but not for the ranking itself; [Google says directly](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) there's no paying for a better local ranking.

## Do you need a storefront to get one?

You don't. I run my own business from home, and it has a profile. Google calls this a service-area business: you list the areas you serve, your home address comes into it only for verification, and it stays off the public profile. What Google does require is a business that shows up in the real world, whether you visit customers, deliver to them, or meet them at their office. If you have that, describe it accurately when you're asked. If your whole business happens online, this isn't the tool for you.

Be ready to prove who you are, though. When I set up my own profile this month, Google's verification was a video: you record a walkthrough showing where you work, plus a business document — a permit, an invoice, or a utility bill — that matches the name on your profile. That last part was the catch for me. A household bill in someone else's name doesn't count, so before you file, find the document that carries the exact name you're registering under.

## A few things I learned doing this work

These are the details that surprised me, and they'll save you some head-scratching.

**Google picks your main photo.** You can set a cover photo, but Google doesn't guarantee it will show first. It may pick a different image for the top spot, including one a customer uploaded. If there are team headshots on the profile, one of them can end up as the banner, cropped to a wide shape, which on a portrait photo means someone's face cut off at the forehead. A purpose-built landscape cover photo is the fix.

**Some fields may only show on phones.** When I checked a profile I'd just filled in, in August 2026, the services and their descriptions appeared on mobile but not in the desktop panel. Before you decide something "didn't work," look at your profile the way customers do, on a phone.

**Be careful with the name field.** [Google's guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) require your real-world name, and extra keywords stuffed into it aren't permitted; their policy says that "could result in" suspension. But Google also says that after a name change you might need to verify the business again. If your name needs correcting, fill in everything else first, while the profile is stable, then change the name as its own deliberate step.

**Check it logged out.** The manager view is not what the public sees. Open an incognito window, search your business by name, and read the panel as a stranger would.

## Go look at yours

Do that last step today. Search your own business, logged out, on your phone. Does the panel say what you sell? Is the photo one you'd have chosen? Are the hours right? Then search "best" plus what you do and your city, and look at whose profiles hold the answer, and how many reviews they have.

If the panel is thin or stale, the business isn't the problem. The record has just gone untended, and most of it is fixable in an afternoon.
